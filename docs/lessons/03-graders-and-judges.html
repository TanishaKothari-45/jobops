Lesson 03 · Agent Env Lab

Graders and Judges

Every way there is to decide whether an output was good — from string matching to model juries — how the real benchmarks do it, where each one breaks, and how to prove your judge can be trusted.

The long one~40 minBuilt to be defended out loud

3.1 The grader is the hard part

People think the hard part of an eval is writing the tasks. It isn't. Tasks are just realistic inputs, and you can get those from production traffic.

The hard part is the grader, because the grader is your definition of correct. Everything downstream inherits it. If the grader is wrong, then every number you report is wrong, every decision you make from those numbers is wrong, and — worst of all — your agent will start optimising toward whatever your grader actually measures rather than what you meant.

In plain words

A school can survive a badly worded exam question. It cannot survive a badly designed marking scheme. One bad question costs a few marks; a bad marking scheme quietly teaches ten thousand students the wrong thing, because they study for the marking scheme.

So this lesson is long on purpose. Graders are where interviews go deep, because they're where production goes wrong.

3.2 The full taxonomy — seven families

Lesson 1 gave you three families as a simplification. Here is the real map. In any evaluation you build, you are choosing from these seven, and usually combining several.

1 · Exact and fuzzy string matching

Does the output equal the answer, contain it, or match a regex? Use for: classification labels, extraction, multiple choice, anything with one right token. Breaks when: there are many correct phrasings. "New Delhi" vs "Delhi" vs "new delhi".

2 · Structural / schema validation

Is the output valid JSON? Does it match the schema? Are all required fields present and correctly typed? Use for: any structured-output feature, tool calls, API responses. Underrated: this catches a huge share of production failures for almost no effort, and it is a hard check — no ambiguity. Always add it before anything cleverer.

3 · Executable / functional correctness

Run the output and see if it works. Unit tests pass, the SQL returns the right rows, the script exits zero. Use for: code generation, SQL, config, anything executable. This is the gold standard where it applies — objective, no opinion, impossible to argue with. It is why coding benchmarks are the most trusted benchmarks we have.

4 · State comparison

Ignore the text entirely; look at what changed in the world. Did the database end up correct? Use for: tool-using agents, anything that takes actions. This is what we built in Project 1, and it is what τ-bench uses. It converts "was the agent good?" — an opinion — into "is the database in state X?" — a fact.

5 · Model-based judging (LLM-as-judge)

Ask another model to score the output against a rubric. Use for: genuinely subjective quality — tone, helpfulness, writing, summarisation faithfulness. Cost: money, latency, variance, and a set of biases we'll cover in detail.

6 · Learned graders / reward models

A model fine-tuned specifically to grade, rather than a general chat model prompted to grade. Reward models in RLHF are exactly this. Open judge models like Prometheus and JudgeLM exist for teams that can't send data to a frontier API. Use for: high volume, privacy constraints, or when you have enough human labels to train on. Cost: you now have a second model to maintain and evaluate.

7 · Human

A person applies a rubric. Use for: creating your first labelled set, validating every other grader, and adjudicating disagreements. Never skip entirely — humans are not just the slowest grader, they are the measuring stick the other six are calibrated against.

The family that mostly died

Statistical overlap metrics — BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, and to a lesser extent BERTScore and embedding cosine similarity. These compare your output to a reference by word or vector overlap. They were the standard for a decade in translation and summarisation, and they correlate poorly with human judgement on open-ended generation, because a good answer worded differently scores badly and a bad answer worded similarly scores well.

Know the names: interviewers over 30 will mention them, and "we moved off ROUGE because overlap doesn't measure correctness" is a good answer. They still have narrow uses — translation, and as a cheap regression tripwire.

3.3 How to actually choose

A decision procedure you can recite. Work down the list and stop at the first one that applies.

  1. Can I execute it? → functional correctness. Run the tests.
  2. Did it change a system? → state comparison. Diff the world.
  3. Is the output structured? → schema validation, always, as a floor.
  4. Is there one right answer in text? → string or fuzzy matching.
  5. Is it genuinely subjective? → LLM judge — and then calibrate it.
  6. Is it subjective, high-volume, and private? → consider a learned grader.
  7. None of the above? → humans, and you probably have a product-definition problem rather than an eval problem.

The single most common mistake in the field is jumping to step 5 when steps 1 to 4 would have worked. An LLM judge feels like the modern answer. It is usually the lazy one.

In plain words

A doctor ordering tests. Temperature, blood pressure, a blood panel — cheap, objective, repeatable, and they answer most questions. You only order the expensive scan with the ambiguous read when the cheap instruments genuinely can't tell you. Nobody starts with the scan.

3.5 LLM-as-judge, mechanically

When you do reach for a judge, these are the design decisions. Each one moves the numbers.

Pointwise vs pairwise vs reference-based

ModeYou askGood forWeakness
Pointwise"Score this answer 1–5 on accuracy."Absolute tracking over time; CI gates.Judges compress scores — almost everything becomes a 4. Poor at detecting small changes.
Pairwise"Which of A and B is better?"Comparing two versions. Far more sensitive.Position bias. No absolute number, so you can't say "we're at 82%".
Reference-based"Here is the correct answer. Does this match it?"When you have gold answers. Much more reliable than free judging.You need gold answers, which is the expensive part.

Rule of thumb: pairwise when comparing two candidates, reference-based when you have gold data, pointwise only when you need a number that persists across time. And if you use pairwise, always run both orderings and average — that alone kills most position bias for double the cost.

Rubric decomposition

Never ask for "quality, 1–10". Split it into named, independently-scored dimensions with explicit criteria. For a follow-up email: is it addressed to the right company · does it reference the actual role · is the tone professional without being obsequious · is it under 120 words.

Three things improve at once: the judge gets more consistent, you learn which dimension failed, and a human can check the judge's work because the criteria are concrete.

In plain words

Compare two marking schemes. "Give the essay a grade out of 10" — two examiners will disagree wildly. "2 marks for a clear thesis, 3 for evidence, 2 for structure, 3 for style" — suddenly they agree, and the student knows what went wrong. Nothing about the essay changed. The scheme did.

The other levers

  • Reasoning before scoring. Ask the judge to state its reasoning first, then the score. Generally improves agreement, and gives you something auditable when you disagree with it.
  • Few-shot anchoring. Show the judge two or three pre-scored examples, especially borderline ones. This is the cheapest accuracy win available and almost nobody does it.
  • Structured output. Force the judge to emit JSON with a fixed schema. Free-text scores are a parsing nightmare and silently fail.
  • Juries. Several cheaper models voting often beats one expensive judge, and it dissolves self-preference bias because no single model's own output is being flattered. The trade is cost and complexity.
  • A different model than the one you're testing. Non-negotiable if you can manage it — self-preference is real.

3.6 Every way a judge lies to you

Position bias

The judge prefers whichever answer it sees first (or last). MT-Bench measured this at roughly 10–15 points of win-rate swing purely from ordering. Fix: run both orders, average. If they disagree, call it a tie.

Verbosity / length bias

Longer answers score higher regardless of quality. Studies have found LLM judges showing a stronger length preference than humans do — one recent analysis put it at about +17% for LLM judges against +13% for humans, indicating a systematic overvaluation rather than merely inherited human taste. Notably, more recent work suggests this has shrunk considerably in current model generations — so treat it as something to measure on your own judge rather than assume. Fix: the AlpacaEval approach — model the effect and control for it, or cap output length in the rubric.

Self-preference / self-enhancement

A model rates its own outputs more favourably than other models' outputs. Fix: judge with a different family than the one you're evaluating, or use a jury.

Sycophancy and framing

If your prompt hints at the answer you want — "did the assistant do a good job here?" — the judge obliges. Fix: neutral framing, and never tell the judge which system produced which answer.

Score compression / leniency

On a 1–10 scale, judges cluster everything between 6 and 9. You lose most of your resolution. Fix: fewer points on the scale (1–4 beats 1–10), explicit criteria per point, or switch to pairwise.

Formatting bias

Markdown, bullet points and headers read as "high effort" to a judge. Fix: say so in the rubric, and check it by running the same content with and without formatting.

Weak at the things it's weak at

A judge is a language model. It is unreliable at arithmetic, at counting, at long-document recall, and at verifying facts it doesn't know. If your rubric asks it to check a number, you have used the wrong grader family — that's a Python check.

The meta-point to carry into an interview

Every one of these is measurable. Position bias: swap the order and compare. Verbosity: correlate score against length. Self-preference: judge outputs from several models including the judge's own family. Leniency: plot the score distribution.

"How would you handle judge bias?" has a weak answer (list the biases) and a strong answer (here is how I'd measure each one on my own judge, and what I'd do about it). The second is what separates candidates.

3.7 Calibration: proving your judge works

This is the section that makes you unusual. Almost everyone builds a judge. Almost nobody validates one.

The procedure

  1. Write the rubric first, precisely enough that another person could apply it.
  2. Hand-label 100 examples yourself against that rubric. Sample them deliberately — include the hard and borderline ones, not 100 easy passes.
  3. Get a second human to label 30 of them. Human–human agreement is your ceiling. If two careful people only agree 75% of the time, a judge hitting 75% is doing as well as a person, and no prompt engineering will take you past it.
  4. Run the judge on the same 100 and compare.
  5. Iterate on the rubric, not the labels. Where the judge disagrees with you, ask whether the rubric was ambiguous. Usually it was.

How to measure agreement, without the maths

Raw agreement is just "what fraction of the time did we say the same thing?" It's intuitive and it flatters you, because some agreement happens by luck. If 90% of your examples are passes and the judge says "pass" to everything, you get 90% raw agreement from a judge that isn't reading.

Cohen's kappa fixes that. In one sentence: kappa is how much better than lucky guessing your agreement is, on a scale where 0 is no better than chance and 1 is perfect. You do not need the formula — every stats library computes it, and interviewers want the interpretation, not the arithmetic.

KappaRead it asWhat to do
below 0.4Your judge is close to guessing.Don't ship it. The rubric is probably ambiguous.
0.4 – 0.6Moderate. Usable as a rough tripwire.Fine for "did something break", not for "is v2 better".
0.6 – 0.8Substantial. This is a working judge.Ship it, report the kappa alongside every score.
above 0.8Strong — or your task is easy.Check you aren't only testing easy cases.

Two related names worth knowing: Krippendorff's alpha, which handles more than two raters and missing labels, and Spearman correlation, which is what you use when your judge outputs a ranking rather than a label — as in AlpacaEval's 0.94 → 0.98 against human rankings.

In plain words

A new smoke alarm. Raw agreement is "it agreed with reality 99% of the time" — which a brick also achieves, since there's usually no fire. Kappa asks the real question: given how rare fires are, is this thing actually detecting them, or is it just quiet?

Recalibrate, or it rots

A calibration is a statement about one judge model, one rubric version, and one data distribution. Change the judge model, change the rubric, or let your traffic drift, and it no longer holds. Store the kappa with the judge version, and re-run the calibration whenever any of the three changes. Treat it like a test that can go stale.

3.8 Partial credit and reward shaping

A binary pass/fail on a twelve-step task tells you almost nothing. "It failed" is not a bug report, and a suite of binary scores can't tell you whether v2 is closer than v1.

Ways to give partial credit, roughly in order of how much you should trust them:

  • Sub-goal checklists. Break the task into required state changes and score the fraction achieved. Objective, and it tells you which sub-goal fails.
  • Weighted checks. Not all checks matter equally — "didn't send an unapproved email" should outweigh "subject line mentions the role". Weight them explicitly rather than pretending they're equal.
  • Milestone / progress scoring. How far through the intended trajectory did it get before going wrong? Useful for long tasks.
  • Graded rubric dimensions from a judge. Most informative, least trustworthy. Calibrate before believing.
The danger of shaping

Every partial-credit scheme is an incentive, and agents optimise incentives. Award points for "created a draft" and you will get empty drafts. Award points per sub-goal and an agent may learn to hit the cheap sub-goals and skip the expensive one. The more granular your reward, the more surface area for gaming — so pair every partial-credit scheme with negative assertions and the occasional hand-read of perfect scores.

3.9 Turning grades into numbers you can trust

You graded 200 runs. Now what do you report?

  • pass@k — at least one of k attempts succeeded. A capability measure: can the model do this at all? Right for code generation where you can test and retry.
  • pass^k — all k attempts succeeded. A reliability measure: will it do this every time? Right for agents taking real actions, where one bad run out of eight is a refunded customer. τ-bench's GPT-4o result — strong pass@1, ~25% pass^8 — is the whole argument for this metric in one number.
  • Mean score with variance across seeds. Run everything at least three times. Report the spread. A single-run number is not a measurement.
  • Paired comparison. When comparing v1 and v2, run both on the same items and compare per-item. This removes most of the noise that comes from some items simply being harder.
  • Confidence intervals. You don't need the maths — bootstrap is the technique and it means "resample your results many times to see how much the average wobbles". If the intervals for v1 and v2 overlap heavily, you haven't shown an improvement.
  • Per-category breakdown. One aggregate number hides everything. Score by task type, by difficulty, by failure mode.
  • Cost and latency alongside quality. "+3% at 2.4× the cost" is the actual decision a team makes.
In plain words

A bowler taking 5 wickets in one match is pass@1 — they can do it. Taking 3+ wickets in eight consecutive matches is pass^8 — they're someone you pick for the tour. Selectors care about the second. So do the people deploying your agent.

3.10 Grading a trajectory, not just an answer

Agents add a dimension. Beyond "was the end state right", you can grade the path:

  • Policy assertions — the process grading from Lesson 1. Did it follow the rules? Machine-checkable from the audit log.
  • Tool-call correctness — right tool, right arguments. Gradeable against a reference trajectory where one exists.
  • Efficiency — steps, tokens and cost against the minimum required. A correct answer in 40 steps is a different product from one in 4.
  • Recovery — when a tool failed, did it adapt or give up? Deliberately break a tool and see.
  • Termination — did it stop, or did the step limit stop it? "Ran out of steps" is its own failure category.
  • First divergence — the single most useful debugging artefact: the earliest step where this run departs from a correct one.

And the pairing that matters most, from Lesson 1: run outcome and process grading together and look at the disagreements. Right outcome with a policy violation is the dangerous quadrant. Wrong outcome with a clean process usually means your tools are broken, not your agent.

3.11 Anti-gaming, concretely

  1. Grade the real outcome, never a proxy. "A draft exists" is a proxy. "A draft exists, names the right company, and references the role" is closer.
  2. Always include negative assertions. SWE-bench's PASS_TO_PASS is one. Ours is emails_sent: 0.
  3. Hold out tasks the agent's developers never see — you can't tune to what you can't read.
  4. Hand-read the perfect scores. Nobody investigates a pass, which is exactly why hacks live there.
  5. Version everything. A score is meaningless without the dataset hash, the prompt version, the judge model and the grader version beside it.
  6. Re-audit the grader periodically. SWE-bench went two years before someone checked whether the tests were fair, and the answer was no.

3.13 Interview answers, out loud

"Walk me through how you'd evaluate an agent."
Start from what's decidable. If it produces code, run the tests. If it takes actions, diff the resulting state. If it emits structure, validate the schema. Only what's left — tone, writing quality — goes to an LLM judge, and that judge gets calibrated against my own hand labels before I trust a number from it. Then I grade the trajectory too, not just the end state, because policy violations don't show up in the outcome. And I never quote a single-run number.
"How do you know your LLM judge is right?"
I hand-label around 100 examples against the rubric, have a second person label 30 of those to establish the human ceiling, then measure judge–human agreement with Cohen's kappa. Below 0.4 I don't ship it — usually the rubric is ambiguous rather than the judge being stupid. Above roughly 0.6 I'll use it, and I report the kappa next to every score so people know what the number is worth. I re-run the calibration whenever the judge model or the rubric changes.
"What biases do LLM judges have, and what do you do about them?"
Position, verbosity, self-preference, sycophancy, score compression and formatting bias. The important part is that each is measurable on my own judge: swap the order and diff for position, correlate score against length for verbosity, judge several model families including the judge's own for self-preference, plot the distribution for compression. Then fix what I find — average both orderings, control for length the way AlpacaEval does, judge with a different family. Prompting "please ignore length" is not a fix.
"Your eval score went from 71 to 74. Ship it?"
Not on that alone. I'd want it run over several seeds with the spread reported, paired on the same items so item difficulty isn't doing the work, and confidence intervals — if they overlap heavily, there's no result. Then I'd check the held-out slice moved too, and look at per-category breakdown, because a 3-point average can hide a regression in one category. And I'd want the cost and latency delta next to it.
"Give me an example of a grader being wrong."
SWE-bench. Fully automated and executable — about as objective as grading gets — and it still had three systematic defects: unit tests so specific they rejected correct patches, issue descriptions too underspecified to define correctness, and environment failures that failed valid solutions for unrelated reasons. OpenAI had humans audit it and released SWE-bench Verified, 500 samples confirmed gradeable. Scores rose without models improving. It's the best reminder that "objective" and "correct" aren't the same thing.
"What's the difference between pass@k and pass^k?"
pass@k is at least one of k attempts succeeding — a capability measure, right for code where you can test and retry. pass^k is all k succeeding — a reliability measure, right for agents that take real actions. τ-bench showed GPT-4o around 25% on pass^8 in retail, roughly a 60% drop from pass^1. An agent that looks capable once can be a coin flip when you need it eight times, and that gap is invisible if you only report pass@1.
"When would you NOT use an LLM judge?"
Whenever something cheaper is decidable — execution, state diff, schema validation, exact match. Also anywhere the judgement involves arithmetic, counting, or verifying facts the judge doesn't know, because those are exactly the things language models are bad at. And in a CI gate that must be fast, cheap and deterministic, a judge's variance alone can trip the threshold.

3.14 What this means for our project

Decisions from this lesson
  • Keep grading LLM-free as the default. State comparison plus policy checks already cover everything decidable in our domain. That's step 2 of the decision procedure, and we should not be at step 5 yet.
  • Add partial credit via sub-goal checklists with explicit weights — "didn't send unapproved" weighs more than "subject mentions the role". Our graders currently treat every check equally, which is a simplification we should fix.
  • Add efficiency and termination to the trajectory grade: steps used against minimum needed, and whether it stopped itself or the limit stopped it.
  • Add exactly one judge, later, for the one undecidable thing: is this follow-up draft actually well written and appropriate? Reference-based, rubric-decomposed into four named criteria, structured JSON output, judged by a different model family.
  • Then calibrate it properly — you hand-label 100 drafts, we measure kappa, and we publish that number in the README. That single artefact is worth more in an interview than the rest of the harness.
  • Report pass^3, not just pass@1, once the LLM agent lands, since our agent takes real actions.
